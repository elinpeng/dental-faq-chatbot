# Sensitive Teeth and Dental Erosion

## Why are my teeth sensitive?
Sensitive teeth feel a short, sharp pain from cold, heat, sweet or sour food, or when you breathe in cold air. The most common cause is exposed dentin. Dentin is the layer under the enamel and contains tiny channels that lead to the nerve of the tooth. When the dentin is exposed, cold and other triggers reach the nerve.

Dentin can become exposed because of:
- Receding gums that expose the root surface
- Brushing too hard or with a hard toothbrush
- Dental erosion (acid wearing away the enamel)
- Teeth grinding
- Gum disease
- A cracked tooth or a broken filling
- Recent teeth whitening or dental treatment (this sensitivity is usually temporary)

## How can I treat sensitive teeth at home?
- Use a toothpaste for sensitive teeth every day. It can take two to four weeks before it works.
- Rub a small amount of sensitive toothpaste onto the sensitive area with your finger before bed and do not rinse.
- Use a soft toothbrush and brush gently with small movements.
- Cut down on acidic food and drinks.
- Use fluoride toothpaste, and ask about an extra fluoride rinse.

## When should I see a dentist for sensitive teeth?
See a dentist if the sensitivity is getting worse, if it lasts a long time after the trigger is gone, if one specific tooth hurts, or if the pain comes without any trigger. These can be signs of a cavity, a crack, or an inflamed nerve that needs treatment. The dentist can also apply a fluoride varnish or a sealing material on the sensitive surfaces.

## What is dental erosion?
Dental erosion is when acid dissolves the enamel of the teeth. Unlike cavities, erosion is not caused by bacteria but by acid that comes directly from food, drinks, or the stomach. Enamel that is lost does not grow back. Erosion has become more common in Sweden, especially among young people.

## What causes dental erosion?
- Frequent intake of acidic drinks such as soft drinks (also sugar-free ones), energy drinks, sports drinks, juice, smoothies, and flavored water
- Acidic foods such as citrus fruits and sour candy
- Acid reflux (stomach acid coming up into the mouth)
- Frequent vomiting, for example in eating disorders or during pregnancy
- Dry mouth, since saliva normally protects against acid

## What are the signs of dental erosion?
- Teeth become sensitive
- The teeth look more yellow because the enamel gets thinner
- The edges of the front teeth become thin and almost see-through
- The chewing surfaces of the back teeth get small hollows
- Fillings seem to stick up above the tooth surface

## How can I prevent dental erosion?
- Drink water when you are thirsty, and have acidic drinks only with meals
- Do not sip acidic drinks for a long time; drink them quickly, preferably through a straw
- Do not swish acidic drinks around in your mouth
- Rinse your mouth with water after something acidic
- Wait about an hour after acidic food or drinks before brushing, so you do not brush away softened enamel
- Use fluoride toothpaste, which makes the enamel more resistant
- Chew sugar-free gum to increase saliva
- Talk to a doctor if you have acid reflux or frequent vomiting

## How is dental erosion treated?
Mild erosion is treated by changing habits and using extra fluoride so it does not get worse. If a lot of enamel has been lost, the dentist can build up the teeth with composite filling material, and in severe cases crowns may be needed. Treatment of erosion is covered by högkostnadsskyddet.
